Urolithin A: Mitophagy, Mitochondrial Quality & Muscle Aging

Urolithin A sits at the intersection of the gut microbiome, mitochondrial quality control and age-related muscle decline. Unlike many longevity compounds, it has moved beyond animal research—but the human evidence still requires careful interpretation.

The short version: Urolithin A is a gut-microbiome-derived metabolite associated with mitochondrial quality control. Human trials support biological activity and suggest possible benefits for selected muscle endurance and mitochondrial biomarkers, but effects on whole-body physical performance have been inconsistent.

What Is Urolithin A?

Urolithin A is a metabolite produced by certain gut bacteria after exposure to ellagitannins and ellagic acid—polyphenolic compounds found in foods such as pomegranates, walnuts and some berries.

The important distinction is that foods do not contain meaningful amounts of Urolithin A itself. They contain precursors. Whether an individual efficiently converts those precursors into Urolithin A depends in part on the composition of the gut microbiome.

This biological variability helped create interest in direct Urolithin A supplementation: it bypasses the requirement for a particular microbial conversion profile.

Why Mitochondria Matter in Aging Muscle

Mitochondria generate much of the ATP required for muscle contraction and cellular maintenance. They are also dynamic structures that accumulate damage and must continually be repaired, recycled or removed.

With aging, mitochondrial quality control becomes less efficient. Dysfunctional mitochondria can accumulate while skeletal muscle progressively loses strength, endurance and metabolic flexibility.

This is where Urolithin A becomes interesting.

Mitophagy: Cellular Quality Control

Mitophagy is the selective form of autophagy used to identify and remove dysfunctional mitochondria. Instead of simply attempting to create more mitochondria, the cell removes damaged components so that the mitochondrial network can be renewed and maintained.

Urolithin A→ Mitophagy signaling→ Removal of damaged mitochondria→ Mitochondrial quality control→ Muscle resilience

Preclinical work identified Urolithin A as an inducer of mitophagy and reported improved muscle function in aging animal models. Human studies subsequently found changes in mitochondrial gene expression and circulating metabolic biomarkers consistent with biological activity.

Important distinction: “supports mitophagy” does not automatically mean “creates more mitochondria,” increases ATP production, or reverses aging. Human trials have not consistently demonstrated those downstream outcomes.

The Human Evidence

Urolithin A is unusual among popular longevity compounds because randomized controlled human trials exist.

One major double-blind trial enrolled 66 adults aged 65 to 90 years and compared 1,000 mg/day of Urolithin A with placebo for four months. Researchers measured walking performance, muscle endurance, ATP production and circulating biomarkers.

The primary outcomes deserve careful attention. Urolithin A did not significantly outperform placebo for improvement in six-minute walking distance or maximal ATP production.

However, muscle-specific endurance improved significantly at two months in both hand and leg testing. Researchers also observed changes in acylcarnitines, ceramides and inflammatory biomarkers consistent with altered mitochondrial and metabolic health.

By four months, endurance continued to improve in the Urolithin A group, but improvement in the placebo group reduced the between-group difference.

What the Broader Human Evidence Says

A 2024 systematic review evaluated five human studies involving approximately 250 healthy participants. Across those studies, Urolithin A altered some mitochondrial, autophagy, fatty-acid-oxidation and inflammatory markers, while selected measures of muscle strength and endurance improved.

At the same time, the review found no clear effect on anthropometric outcomes, cardiovascular measures or overall physical function and no demonstrated improvement in maximal mitochondrial ATP production.

A newer 2026 systematic review of randomized controlled trials reached an even more cautious conclusion: pooled six-minute-walk results were directionally favorable but statistically inconclusive, with low certainty of evidence. Findings for strength, endurance and mitochondrial biomarkers were considered exploratory rather than reproducible proof of efficacy.

Muscle Aging: Where Urolithin A Fits

Age-related muscle decline is not caused by one pathway. Muscle mass, neuromuscular signaling, protein intake, physical activity, hormones, inflammation, vascular health and mitochondrial quality all contribute.

Urolithin A is therefore better viewed as a potential mitochondrial quality-control intervention than as a replacement for resistance training or adequate nutrition.

The biologically interesting question is whether improving the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria can help aging muscle maintain function over time. Current human research suggests that this hypothesis is plausible, but not yet conclusively demonstrated at the level of meaningful whole-body performance.

Food Precursors vs Direct Urolithin A

Pomegranate and other ellagitannin-rich foods remain valuable components of a healthy diet, but eating them does not guarantee a specific Urolithin A exposure. Gut microbiome composition determines whether—and how efficiently—the precursors are converted.

Direct supplementation removes that conversion variable. That makes supplementation useful for controlled research because investigators can deliver a known amount of Urolithin A regardless of an individual's microbial phenotype.

Safety and Tolerability

Published human studies to date have generally reported Urolithin A as well tolerated over the studied durations. In the four-month older-adult randomized trial, adverse events did not differ significantly between the Urolithin A and placebo groups, and no serious adverse events were reported.

That evidence is reassuring within the populations, doses and durations studied. It does not establish lifetime safety or safety across every medical condition, medication combination or population.

What Urolithin A Is — and What It Isn't

What the evidence supports

  • Urolithin A is a microbiome-derived metabolite of ellagitannin/ellagic-acid metabolism.
  • It has substantial mechanistic evidence involving mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy.
  • Human randomized trials demonstrate biological activity.
  • Selected studies report improvements in muscle endurance and mitochondrial-associated biomarkers.
  • Short-term human studies have generally found it well tolerated.

What remains uncertain

  • It has not been shown to reverse human aging.
  • It is not established as a treatment for sarcopenia.
  • Whole-body physical-performance benefits remain inconsistent.
  • Human studies have not consistently shown increased maximal ATP production.
  • Long-term clinical outcomes and optimal populations remain unresolved.

The Longevity Question

Urolithin A is frequently placed in the “longevity supplement” category. That description should be used carefully.

Improving a biological process associated with aging is not equivalent to demonstrating lifespan extension. There is currently no evidence that Urolithin A extends human lifespan.

Its value to longevity research is more specific: Urolithin A gives researchers a practical way to investigate whether targeting mitochondrial quality control can preserve function during aging.

ProtokolX Assessment

Urolithin A has a stronger human evidence base than many compounds promoted in the longevity space—but the strongest claims are still ahead of the data.

The case for Urolithin A begins with mitochondrial quality control. Preclinical research strongly supports mitophagy-related biology, while controlled human studies demonstrate measurable biological activity and some encouraging muscle-endurance findings.

The limitations matter just as much: primary physical-performance outcomes have sometimes failed to separate from placebo, ATP-production findings are not consistently positive, and recent systematic review evidence rates the clinical performance signal as uncertain.

The useful question is not whether Urolithin A “reverses aging.” It is whether better mitochondrial housekeeping can help preserve muscle function as we age. Human research has begun answering that question—but the answer is not finished.
